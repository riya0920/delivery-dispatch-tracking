`updates_per_query` is the ratio that decides everything: couriers ping
every 1-5 seconds, so at 10,000 couriers the write load is thousands per
second while dispatch queries arrive at a far lower rate. The realistic
regime is the BOTTOM block, not the top.

THE CHOICE, and it changes with the ratio:
  at   5 updates/query -> grid_hash     (blended cost 0.638 ms per query
                            incl. its updates; linear_scan 1.412)
  at  50 updates/query -> kdtree        (blended cost 1.220 ms per query
                            incl. its updates; linear_scan 1.923)
  at 200 updates/query -> grid_hash     (blended cost 2.368 ms per query
                            incl. its updates; linear_scan 2.549)

Reading the columns:
  kdtree     fastest QUERIES, worst UPDATES -- it rebuilds. It also
             carries recall below 1.0, because between rebuilds it is
             answering from stale positions. That is the amortisation
             showing up as a CORRECTNESS cost, which is the honest way
             to account for it.
  grid_hash  O(1) updates, always fresh, query scans a ring of cells.
  hex_h3     same shape; hexagons have six equidistant neighbours, so a
             k-ring is a real radius instead of a square whose corners
             are 1.41x further than its edges. On a square grid the set
             of couriers considered depends on where in the cell the
             restaurant happens to sit.

THE RESULT I DID NOT EXPECT, AND IT NEEDED A SCALE SWEEP TO INTERPRET:
at 200 updates per query the NAIVE LINEAR SCAN wins on blended cost. A
vectorised haversine over 10,000 points is a single numpy call, while
every index pays per-update Python overhead to maintain itself. At a
high enough write:read ratio, maintaining an index costs more than not
having one.

That is true and it does not generalise, which the fleet-size sweep shows:

  query cost (ms) by fleet size, at 50 updates/query:
index   grid_hash  linear_scan  scan/grid ratio
fleet                                          
2000       0.4942       0.5723           1.1579
10000      0.8314       2.1021           2.5286

200000     6.6384      65.5004           9.8669

  The scan is LINEAR in fleet size; the grid is not -- it touches a
  fixed ring of cells whose occupancy grows only with DENSITY. The
  ratio in the last column is the whole argument, and it is why the
  answer at 10,000 couriers in one metro is not the answer at 500,000.
  Benchmarking at the size you have and deploying at the size you will
  have is how teams end up rewriting dispatch.

SUBSTITUTION WARNING, so nobody quotes these as database numbers:
PostGIS, Redis and h3 are not installed here. These are the ALGORITHMIC
SHAPES in-process -- no network hop, no serialisation, no concurrency
control, no durability. A real PostGIS query pays milliseconds of round
trip that dwarf everything measured above. What transfers is how read
and write costs SCALE and what staleness each structure forces.

AT 500K COURIERS ACROSS 50 METROS the answer changes shape entirely:
one global index becomes a sharding problem. Shard by geo cell, route
queries by cell id, and accept that cross-shard queries at metro borders
need scatter-gather. The hex index is the one that survives that move
because the cell id IS the shard key and neighbour lookup is arithmetic
rather than a range scan. Not built here.

2. EXACTLY-ONE-ASSIGNMENT UNDER CONCURRENT ACCEPTS
==============================================================================
500 orders, 3000 offers (6 per order), 24 threads accepting concurrently.
  offers accepted (wins)      : 500
  orders assigned             : 500 / 500
  losers that raced and lost  : 2490
  invariant violations        : none

Every order has exactly one assignment and exactly one accepted offer,
and no courier holds two orders. The invariant is enforced by a single
guarded compare-and-set on the order's assignment under one lock -- NOT
by check-then-write, which is the race it exists to prevent. It is the
same defect as decrement-and-hope inventory in SE-1, in a different
costume.

TRACING TWO ACCEPTS 40ms APART: the first takes the lock, sees
assigned_to is None, writes its courier id, marks its own offer accepted
and marks every other open offer on that order SUPERSEDED. The second
takes the lock, finds assigned_to set, marks its offer superseded, and
returns False -- so the courier gets 'this order was just taken' rather
than silence. 2490 couriers took that path in this run and none of them
corrupted anything.

THE TRADEOFF, PRICED. A longer window buys a better assignment and
charges every courier and customer the wait. `first_offer_latency_s` is
the AVERAGE delay an order sits before anyone is even offered it -- half
the window.

Best batching gain measured: 1.34% on mean pickup distance, at a 60s
window (30s average first-offer delay). That is SMALL, and reporting
it as small is the honest read: with 3490 available couriers in this metro
the nearest courier is already ~0.20 km away, so there is very little
room for a smarter assignment to find a better one. Batching pays when
supply is TIGHT and couriers are far apart -- which is exactly the
regime this fleet is not in. A benchmark run only in the easy regime
would have concluded batching is worthless; the correct conclusion is
that its value is a function of utilisation, and this run does not
sweep utilisation. That sweep is the missing experiment.

PRODUCT SAYS COURIERS CHURN WHEN OFFERS ARE SLOW. That is a real cost
this table cannot see: it measures assignment quality, not courier
retention. The two are measured in different units and the exchange rate
between them is a business input, not an engineering one. What I would
do with these curves: pick the shortest window whose gain is still
material -- the curve flattens, so most of the win arrives early -- and
make the window ADAPTIVE, short when supply is plentiful (greedy is
nearly optimal when everyone is close) and longer only when utilisation
is high and the assignment actually matters. Adaptive windowing is not
built here.

==============================================================================

Overall MAE 10.06 min, bias +3.00 min, p90 abs error 21.42 min.
80% interval coverage 0.494 against a target of 0.80.

THAT IS A FAILING NUMBER AND IT IS THE MOST USEFUL RESULT IN THIS
SECTION. An interval that covers 49% of outcomes while claiming 80%
is not a conservative estimate, it is a WRONG one, and it fails in the
direction that costs support tickets: the customer is told 25-31 minutes
and the order arrives at 44. The base sigma was picked by hand, which is
exactly how this happens.

The fix is to calibrate sigma from residuals rather than assert it.
Fitted on the first half of the evaluation set, scored on the second:

  calibrated base sigma: 12.41 min (hand-picked was 4.00)
               n     mae   bias  p90_abs  coverage_80
seg                                                  
far         1473  10.441  2.969   21.893        0.860
far_stale    340  10.057  3.491   21.085        0.991
mid          118   5.468  0.707   11.166        0.975
mid_stale     40   5.933  2.519   11.391        1.000
near          23   4.925  0.803    9.914        1.000
near_stale     6   5.077  1.542    8.393        1.000

Overall coverage after calibration: 0.894 (target 0.80).

THE STALENESS POLICY. Rows ending `_stale` are predictions made from a
location fix older than 45s. The point ETA on those rows is no better
than on fresh ones -- and a system that renders them identically is
lying at exactly the moment the customer is most likely to be looking.
So the interval WIDENS with the age of the fix, which is why the stale
segments over-cover rather than collapse. Over-covering is the correct
direction to be wrong when you do not know where the courier is.

An ETA is a model with an SLO, not a decoration on a map. The number a
customer stares at for thirty minutes deserves a measured error
distribution, and 'we said 25 minutes' is not a claim anyone can audit
without one.

DRILL C -- the WISMO surface, second by second. A courier's GPS dies in
a tunnel while the customer is watching:

  t+ 10s  state=live           dot=True   Arriving in 12-24 min
  t+ 60s  state=delayed_signal dot=True   Last seen 1 min ago - arriving in 9-27 min
  t+150s  state=delayed_signal dot=True   Last seen 2 min ago - arriving in 3-33 min
  t+400s  state=signal_lost    dot=False  We've lost signal from your courier. Your order is still on its way; we'll update as soon as we hear from them.

At 180s the dot comes OFF the map. That is the whole WISMO principle:
a frozen dot is worse than no dot, because the customer believes the
frozen dot until they stop believing anything you tell them. A
confidently-wrong tracking page generates the exact support ticket it
was built to prevent.

AND THE OTHER DIRECTION -- prep time blows up from 8 to 25 minutes:
the ETA must move, but not whipsaw. smooth_eta() damps DECREASES and
lets INCREASES through almost undamped, because a customer who will be
waiting 20 extra minutes needs to know now, while they can still do
something about it. Good news can wait; bad news cannot.
